It is already late January 2026, which may be a little late to start looking ahead to the year.

Still, with the new semester less than a month away, I feel that someone who has put together all these teaching materials should explain why I keep doing it as technology changes so quickly, and why I still think these things are worth learning.

I hope these words offer a small response or reminder to anyone sitting at their desk, feeling uncertain.

<!-- truncate -->

First, I must admit that the title is a little bit of bait. Coding is my starting point for explaining to people who may be unfamiliar with software development why learning software engineering and developing an engineer's way of thinking still matter in 2026.

All right, let's begin.

This style may feel unfamiliar. It is not how I usually write; it can put people off. But this is not a conventional tech blog post about a tool or a technical discovery. It is an observation about abstract ideas. So this time, I will begin with two fundamental questions:

1. What is the essence of being a software engineer?
2. What is the mechanism, or essence, of delegation?

## What Is the Essence of Being a Software Engineer?

I would not quite call myself a software engineer yet. I think of myself as "someone who tinkers with code." Still, I hope these thoughts give people who are less familiar with this world something to consider, and perhaps start a useful conversation.

If you have done some development, what follows may resonate. If it does not, I hope you remember it and let your future experience test whether my reasoning was roughly right.

Here is the point: "Writing the code is actually the engineer's final step," at least if we set maintenance and operations aside for a moment.

Why do I say that? Because building software involves far more than producing the finished thing. People who have not done development often see only that last step. It is where engineers become the familiar stereotype: sitting at a desk, staring at screens most computer users may never see, occasionally typing furiously as if arguing with someone on LINE.

Even without AI, engineers who type rapidly from beginning to end are probably rare. We often spend more time thinking, discussing, reading code and external documentation, checking whether inputs and outputs behave as expected, and comparing the result with the architecture specification. Some people even say that if an engineer types furiously for a long time, they are probably messaging someone. I mostly agree, although I prefer not to make absolute claims.

Read those last two paragraphs again: even the activities I listed already go well beyond "writing code," don't they?

So equating an engineer's work with writing code is a very incomplete understanding.

In practice, engineers spend more effort on:

1. Clarifying requirements through discussion and checking alignment with the architecture specification
2. Organizing the architecture through thought and reading and writing code

And that is already the implementation stage. Before it, the work may include:

1. Identifying the problem
2. Analyzing the problem
3. Clarifying requirements
4. Designing the architecture

Did you notice that I mentioned requirements and architecture twice? In my admittedly limited view, these are central to software engineering. What do they represent? I think they mean connecting the solution to the real problem and handling that problem efficiently.

Writing code is the act of implementing a solution. The valuable part is the analysis behind it and the creativity and efficiency of the solution itself.

To borrow the fishing analogy teachers often use, **the most valuable part is not pulling a hooked fish out of the water**. It is:

1. Through careful *planning and investigation*, finding a river with plenty of fish and few other anglers
2. Understanding the fish in that river and *choosing suitable* hooks and bait
3. Through *practice*, learning to cast *efficiently* and reel in the catch quickly

People may notice only the fish you pull out, but that is the final step. Much of the success was established by the earlier work.

Returning to my opening bait: learning coding, data structures, and algorithms is much like the third step in this analogy. You learn to use your fishing rod effectively and efficiently.

If coding is the third step, what about the first two? The difficulty is that coding concepts deeply influence them. Without a software-oriented way of thinking, it is hard to approach a problem through software or weigh implementation cost, convenience, and performance across different approaches.

From this perspective, learning to code certainly still has value. But someone may ask:

> AI is already quite good at writing code and analyzing problems. Why should I learn these things?

That brings me to what I see as the problem of delegation.

## The Mechanism and Essence of Delegation

When we use—or perhaps "put to work"—AI to write code, research information, or do other tasks,
we can think of it as hiring an **engineer without a physical presence** to act on our behalf. That absence matters, and I will return to it shortly. This engineer works extremely quickly and learns periodically, taking in a large amount of advanced knowledge at once.

It seems we no longer need to get our own hands dirty. We can ask this engineer to investigate, analyze, design, implement, and check everything. Our role seems to be sending instructions and checking whether the result is good. If it is not, we provide more context, clearer instructions, and so on.

What could go wrong? If we hand everything to AI, we give up practicing software engineering and become project managers instead. Why should that matter? I think we risk forgetting, or abandoning, a way of thinking and solving problems, and ultimately the possibilities it opens up. We may forget that practical problems can be solved this way. Even if we focus narrowly on code, losing control over it is like setting aside our tools and returning to a more primitive way of doing things. When circumstances change, we cannot act ourselves. We can only keep issuing prompts and waiting for AI to solve the problem.

Some readers may still see no problem with that, and that is fine. Let me return to the idea of having no physical presence, which may make the issue clearer.

We share a basic expectation: when something goes wrong, someone must be accountable.

When a company's service goes down, the CTO or department head may prepare for a serious grilling. After being criticized, managers may pass that frustration down the chain. Eventually, someone shrugs and says, "AI wrote it." What happens next?

I do not believe the manager will pat them on the shoulder and say, "Well, nothing we can do." They are more likely to receive even stronger criticism, because someone must be accountable. AI has no physical presence to hold responsible, so the person operating it must take responsibility. To me, **responsibility** is the essence of delegation.

What gives us the ability to take responsibility for AI's output? Knowledge. Nothing else, unless sheer luck counts.

Why knowledge? Because it lets us read and understand what AI produces. We may not be faster than the machine, but we can follow its output, understand it, challenge it, and ultimately say with confidence: this approach works.
Where does that knowledge come from? Continual learning, starting with solid fundamentals. A strong foundation is what gives that confidence substance.

So we return again to the bait: "Why learn to code?"
As long as code remains the underlying means of interacting with computers, the world will need people who can write it and think like software engineers. Someone must ultimately be responsible.

## Closing Thoughts

Will computers stop being based on code in 2026? I cannot give a definitive answer, but I think that is unlikely, probably for the next several years as well. What I do feel certain about is that confidently taking responsibility for AI's output requires more and more knowledge. People who cannot do that may hear, "Well, AI is much more useful than you."

I do not say this to frighten anyone. What looks like a crisis may also be a turning point in a person's life.
Today, much of the world is investing in technology, pouring talent and money into what still looks like fertile ground. But will there really be a world where everyone is an engineer? No. The world needs many kinds of people besides engineers. It is much larger than engineering.
What has AI changed for us? It gives us more opportunities to explore beyond our usual boundaries and more easily gain an initial picture of almost any subject.
At the same time, AI can obscure part of the world. Seeing the fuller picture still takes thought and careful attention to experience, whether we are talking about knowledge, ethics, or the responsibility of being human.

I hope these thoughts help you understand why you are sitting at your desk in 2026.
